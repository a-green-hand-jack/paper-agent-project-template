# Introduction

The introduction should make the reader care about the question, understand the relevant gap, and know what the paper will do about it.

## Build toward a specific gap

A useful introduction narrows progressively:

1. Why the broad problem matters.
2. What is already known.
3. What remains unresolved or inadequate.
4. Why that unresolved point matters.
5. What this paper does to address it.

Do not treat the introduction as a miniature encyclopedia. Background is useful only insofar as it enables the reader to understand the research question and the contribution.

## Distinguish three things

Keep separate:

- **field importance** — why the general topic matters,
- **knowledge gap** — what is not yet known or solved,
- **study objective** — what this paper actually tests, estimates, builds, or explains.

Many weak introductions state the first and third but never make the second precise.

## End with a clear contract

By the end of the introduction, the reader should be able to predict what kind of evidence the results section must deliver.

The final paragraph can briefly state:

- the study objective or hypothesis,
- the approach,
- the major result at a high level.

Do not conceal the paper's direction merely to create suspense. Scientific writing benefits from a clear contract with the reader.

## Audience calibration

Define terms and provide background at the level needed by the target audience. A specialist paper can assume more domain knowledge than an interdisciplinary one.

Avoid forcing the reader to repeatedly decode acronyms or infer why a method, organism, dataset, or benchmark was chosen.

## Introduction diagnostics

Possible structural problems:

- The introduction is long because the study question is still vague.
- Several different gaps are introduced but only one is addressed.
- The most specific gap appears only in the discussion.
- Large blocks of background never affect the interpretation of the results.
- The last paragraph describes activities rather than a scientific objective.

When shortening, cut background that does not change the reader's ability to understand the gap or evaluate the study.
