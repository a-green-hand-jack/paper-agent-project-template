# Results

The Results section should establish the chain of evidence supporting the central contribution.

## Organize by questions and claims

Avoid using the laboratory timeline as the organizing principle. Prefer a sequence in which each subsection establishes a result required by the next.

For each subsection, identify:

- the scientific question,
- the evidence used to answer it,
- the conclusion supported by that evidence,
- how that conclusion advances the overall argument.

A useful subsection title is often a declarative scientific statement rather than a generic label such as "Experiment 2."

## Let results accumulate logically

Common logical patterns include:

- establish a phenomenon → rule out artifacts → test mechanism,
- validate a measurement → apply it → interpret the pattern,
- pose competing explanations → eliminate alternatives → support the remaining explanation,
- establish baseline → introduce perturbation → quantify change.

Choose the sequence that makes the inference easiest to evaluate.

## Separate observation from interpretation

Within Results, distinguish:

- what was measured,
- what the data show,
- what inference is directly supported.

Broader implications, speculation, and field-level interpretation generally belong in Discussion.

Do not merely restate figure contents. Explain the scientific point established by the figure.

## Make evidence inspectable

Report enough information for the reader to understand the analysis and its uncertainty. When statistics are central, make clear:

- what was compared,
- what quantity was estimated,
- uncertainty or variability,
- relevant sample sizes,
- assumptions or model choices that materially affect interpretation.

Do not let a p-value substitute for the scientific result.

## Keep controls near the claims they protect

If a conclusion depends on a control, validation, sensitivity analysis, or robustness check, place it close enough that the reader can evaluate the inference without holding the concern unresolved for many pages.

## Results diagnostics

Watch for:

- figures that are included because they were produced rather than because they support the story,
- conclusions that require data not shown,
- multiple analyses that all answer the same question without adding robustness or scope,
- interpretation that outruns the evidence,
- important negative or contradictory findings silently omitted,
- subsection headings that describe methods instead of findings.
