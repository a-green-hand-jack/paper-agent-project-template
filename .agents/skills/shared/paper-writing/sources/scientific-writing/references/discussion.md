# Discussion

The Discussion should explain what the results mean, how strongly they support that meaning, where the interpretation can fail, and how the work changes the scientific landscape.

## Reconnect to the original gap

Begin by returning to the question established in the introduction. State the main findings in interpretive form and explain how they reduce the original uncertainty.

Do not simply replay the Results section figure-by-figure.

## Separate confidence levels

Use different language for:

- conclusions directly supported by the study,
- plausible interpretations,
- speculative extensions,
- future hypotheses.

When several mechanisms are consistent with the data, do not present one as uniquely established unless alternatives were meaningfully excluded.

## Treat limitations as part of the inference

A limitation is scientifically useful when it tells the reader where the conclusion may not hold.

For each important limitation, ask:

- Which claim does this limitation weaken?
- Does it affect internal validity, generalizability, measurement, statistical precision, or mechanism?
- Is there evidence that bounds the problem?
- What would resolve the uncertainty?

Avoid generic limitation lists disconnected from the paper's claims.

## Compare with prior literature analytically

Do more than say whether previous studies agree or disagree. When findings differ, examine whether the studies differ in population, context, measurement, design, analysis, or operational definition.

A disagreement may reveal a boundary condition rather than a contradiction.

## End with a proportionate significance claim

The final message should be important because of what the evidence supports, not because of adjectives.

Prefer:

- what changed in understanding,
- what new capability exists,
- what uncertainty was removed,
- what new question is now testable.

Avoid claiming that a study is "the first," "definitive," or "transformative" unless the manuscript can substantiate that characterization.

## Discussion diagnostics

Problems often appear when:

- the strongest claim is introduced only here,
- limitations are mentioned but do not change any interpretation,
- the discussion becomes a second literature review,
- future work is used to compensate for missing evidence needed by the current claim,
- correlation is rephrased as mechanism or causation,
- the conclusion generalizes beyond the sampled system without justification.
