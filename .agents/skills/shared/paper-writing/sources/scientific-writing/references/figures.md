# Figures

Treat a scientific figure as an interface between data and a reader. Its job is not to display everything that was analyzed; its job is to make the intended scientific comparison accurately perceivable.

## Design from the message backward

Before choosing a chart type, state:

- who the audience is,
- what question the figure answers,
- what comparison should be visually easy,
- what uncertainty or variation must remain visible.

The same data may require different visual designs for specialists, students, or a general audience.

## Make each figure self-contained enough to inspect

A reader should be able to understand the key elements without hunting through the paper.

Check:

- labels and units,
- definitions of symbols and encodings,
- sample sizes where relevant,
- explanation of error bars or intervals,
- conditions and groups,
- a legend that explains what was done,
- a title or caption that communicates the scientific point.

## Optimize the comparison, not decoration

Use visual encodings that make the important comparison easy. Avoid unnecessary dimensions, ornament, or graphical effects that consume attention without adding information.

When exact values matter, make them recoverable. When patterns matter, emphasize the pattern without hiding variability.

## Preserve scientific honesty

Do not use axis limits, normalization, aggregation, smoothing, or selective panels in ways that exaggerate or hide effects.

If preprocessing materially changes the visual pattern, explain it.

Show raw or individual observations when they are important for judging distribution, heterogeneity, or outliers.

## Figures as a manuscript test

Arrange the main figures in order and ask whether they form a coherent evidence chain. If the figures cannot tell the core story, the manuscript may have a structural problem rather than a prose problem.

## Accessibility

Do not rely on color alone when categories must remain distinguishable. Use redundant cues such as line style, marker shape, direct labels, or luminance differences when appropriate.

Test legibility at realistic publication size, not only while zoomed in.
