# Paper Structure

## Start with the central contribution

Before drafting, force the paper into one sentence:

> This paper shows that ______.

Treat that sentence as a working constraint, not marketing copy. If several unrelated claims compete for the blank, the manuscript probably needs a sharper scope, a hierarchy of claims, or more than one paper.

The central contribution should be visible in the title, abstract, introduction, results sequence, and discussion. Secondary findings may support the contribution, qualify it, or show its scope, but they should not become independent stories that make the reader repeatedly reorient.

## Organize by scientific logic, not project chronology

Research rarely happens in the order that makes the best paper. Failed experiments, detours, and the historical sequence of analyses may matter for the lab notebook but not for the manuscript's main argument.

Build the manuscript around the shortest defensible reasoning path:

1. What important question or gap exists?
2. What did the study do to address it?
3. What evidence was obtained?
4. What does that evidence support?
5. What remains uncertain?
6. Why does the result matter?

If a section exists mainly because "we did this next," reconsider its position.

## Outline before prose

Create an outline in declarative statements rather than topic labels.

Weak outline:

- Experiment 1
- Experiment 2
- Additional analysis

Better outline:

- The phenotype appears only under condition X.
- The effect cannot be explained by measurement artifact Y.
- Perturbing pathway Z removes the effect.

Declarative headings expose missing logical steps early. They also make it easier to see whether the evidence actually accumulates toward the claimed contribution.

## Use figures as the backbone of the argument

A useful manuscript-planning test is to arrange the planned figures and tables in story order before writing full text. A scientifically mature figure sequence should let a knowledgeable reader infer the major reasoning path.

For each figure, state:

- the question it answers,
- the result it establishes,
- why that result is needed for the next step.

If two figures establish the same point, consider merging or removing one. If the argument jumps between figures, identify the missing evidence or explanation.

## Control topic switching

Readers have limited working memory. Avoid repeatedly leaving a topic and returning to it later unless the central contribution itself requires recurrence.

Prefer:

- keeping related evidence together,
- presenting parallel arguments in parallel structure,
- defining technical terms before relying on them,
- minimizing abbreviations that require backtracking.

A manuscript that forces readers to keep many unresolved threads alive will feel harder than the underlying science needs to be.

## Paragraph test

Every paragraph should have a job. Before revising sentences, write a one-line description of that job.

Ask:

- What question does this paragraph answer?
- What new state of understanding should the reader have at the end?
- Does the next paragraph depend on that state?

If a paragraph has no distinct job, move, merge, or remove it.

## Structure diagnostic

A structural problem is likely when any of these are true:

- You cannot explain the manuscript outline aloud in a few minutes.
- The title and abstract emphasize different contributions.
- The introduction promises a question that the results do not directly answer.
- Results are ordered by when analyses happened rather than by inference.
- The discussion introduces the paper's most important interpretation for the first time.
- Multiple sections repeat the same background because the argument keeps changing direction.
- Removing a major paragraph does not change the reader's ability to follow the claim.

Do structural surgery before copyediting.
